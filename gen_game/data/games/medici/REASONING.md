# Reasoning and planning: Medici: Sealed Harbour

**Verdict: adequate.** Winning rewards real reasoning: reading patrons from bids and scores, pricing each lot by its swing against the closest rival, and timing ship fills around your own selling turns. Card order still decides close games, and longer plans would matter more if players could see more of the coming lots or had resources that carry over between days.

From 3 play(s): 318 choices, 182 with a reason given (about 31.9 words each); results {'seat 1': 1, 'seat 3': 1, 'seats 1 and 3': 1}.

## Reasoning: 3 of 3

Most bids and reserves weigh tie-break position, who receives the payment, rivals' bid history and ship totals. Players regularly worked out hidden patrons from bidding behaviour and from end-of-day florin totals, and endgame decisions were often exact outcome calculations comparing buy, lose and discard cases, though a few patron reads were wrong.

- match 1, turn 42, seat 3: "Grain looks like seat 2's patron, since their day-1 florins only add up with a 6-florin Grain bonus." (Infers a hidden patron from public scoring.)
- match 1, turn 97, seat 1: "I rebuilt day 1's scoring. Seat 3 got no bonus for Spice 5 and Spice 4. Dye is mine and Grain is seat 2's, so seat 3's patron must be Cloth." (Deduces a patron by elimination.)

- match 2, turn 106, seat 3: "ties go to me, so this happens whenever seat 1 bids 3 or less and the reserve allows it. I finish on 41 and seat 1 on 39." (Exact case analysis of the final lot.)
- match 3, turn 102, seat 2: "A reserve higher than 3 can only block sales that help me." (Sets the reserve from enumerated outcomes.)

## Planning: 2 of 3

Players often set up later moves. They filled their ship before their own sale so an unsold lot is harmlessly discarded, left slots open for their own selling turn, and used talk to shape a rival's later bid or reserve. Plans stretch over two or three rounds at most, and no multi-turn plan that depends on rivals' cooperation is carried through.

- match 2, turn 23, seat 2: "Once my ship is full, I can sell my own lot next round with no risk of having to load it." (Buys now to make the next selling turn risk-free.)
- match 1, turn 65, seat 1: "It also means that when I sell in round 12, an unsold lot is simply discarded." (Sets up a later high-reserve sale.)
- match 1, turn 95, seat 1: "I can fill my last slot in round 17, when I sell." (Keeps a slot for their own later lot.)
- match 2, turn 96, seat 1: "A firm claim may scare off their bid, and then I can set a low reserve and keep Grain 1 cheaply." (Talk planned to steer a rival's bid before the reserve is chosen.)
- match 3, turn 98, seat 3: "It is my patron card, and it sets up a possible ship-prize win on the last lot." (Cheap buy aimed at a final-round play.)

## Luck: 2 of 3

The order of the lots, and whose selling turn they come up on, decides a lot: patron cards landing on your own sale or on the last lot shaped close finishes. Choices still mattered, because the margins came from overpays and well-priced reserves.

- match 3, turn 104, seat 3: "a bid of 4 or less wins outright. A bid of 5 shares the win with seat 1 at 45." (The final result hinged on a Grain card (seat 3's patron) appearing as the last lot.)
- match 1, turn 102, seat 3: "Owning it also gives me a real shot at first place if a 5 shows up in round 18." (Players' prospects depended on the next draw.)
