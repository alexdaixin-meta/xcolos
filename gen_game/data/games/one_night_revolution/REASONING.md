**Verdict: adequate.** Winning takes real reasoning: schedule deduction from scores, information-optimal investigations and modelling what each Rebel can infer decide most votes, and good tie-engineering earns points. Hiding scoring sub-totals, or leaving the schedule's later rounds less determined, would keep deception and multi-round planning relevant to the end.

## Reasoning: 3 of 3

Most decisions rest on specific, correct inference. Players rebuild the hidden schedule from score changes, rule out used pairs, pick investigations by how much a result would tell them, and catch false arguments from Informants. Rebels also check claims against their own private knowledge ("I know I'm Rebel, so…") and against the remaining pair list, which goes well past restating the rules.

- match 1, turn 64, seat 4: "Since I'm a Rebel, tonight's pair must be 1-2, 1-5, 2-5 or 3-5. If I investigate seat 1 and get "Rebel", only 2-5 and 3-5 remain, so seat 5 must be an Informant." (Uses the used-pair constraint to choose the investigation with the most information value.)
- match 2, turn 20, seat 5: "Seats 3 and 4 both used the same false argument, that two framers can't fake four results. In fact, a frame makes every investigation of seat 1 come back 'Informant'." (Spots an Informant's false logical claim and uses it to vote correctly against the table.)
- match 1, turn 143, seat 3: "Seat 4 gained 0 points that round. As a Rebel voting for seat 2, a Rebel, it would still have earned the 1-point bonus when Informant seat 1 was terminated." (Infers a hidden past role from the scoring arithmetic.)

## Planning: 2 of 3

Players often plan the vote count, building ties on purpose to lock in the Rebel bonus or to shield a partner. Informants choose frames based on what each Rebel can deduce, and a few keep their record clean for later rounds. Most planning still covers only the current round, with little multi-round setup beyond tracking the schedule and the endgame score race.

- match 1, turn 100, seat 5: "If I vote 4, seats 1 and 4 tie and are both terminated. Then an Informant is terminated whichever pair it is, so I get the bonus point for sure" (Engineers a tie, based on predicted votes, to guarantee the bonus.)
- match 1, turn 144, seat 4: "Seat 5 can already rule out 1-5 and will know seat 2 is an Informant. Making seat 1 look like an Informant pushes seats 3 and 5 toward thinking tonight is 1-2" (Frames a target chosen by modelling each Rebel's private deductions.)
- match 1, turn 80, seat 5: "Voting seat 2 too costs me nothing and makes me look like a Rebel in later rounds." (Gives up a partner to build credibility for future rounds.)
- match 1, turn 190, seat 5: "if seat 2 or seat 3 votes for a Rebel, I gain a point and win outright. Neither of them can win anyway, so I'm asking them to break the tie." (Endgame plan that depends on how other players' incentives line up.)

## Luck: 2 of 3

The shuffled schedule matters a lot. Once enough pairs are used, late-round Informants are publicly forced and earn almost nothing, while early Informants act behind more uncertainty. In match 1, seat 1 was a provable Informant in rounds 9 and 10 and finished last on Informant points through no choice of its own.

- match 1, turn 161, seat 1: "Remaining pairs make me provably an Informant this round, so my best chance is to cause confusion." (The schedule position strips the Informant of any deception value.)
- match 1, turn 181, seat 1: "Seat 5 is my partner and is 5 points ahead of me. As fellow Informants we gain the same points this round, so I can't overtake." (The final round is fixed by the schedule, and choices cannot change the ranking.)
