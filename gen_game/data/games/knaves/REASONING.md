# Reasoning and planning: Knaves Short Deck with Bids

**Verdict: adequate.** Winning this game clearly takes reasoning and planning: exact bids reward card counting, hidden-card inference and multi-trick steering, and players also exploit opponents' public bids. It would take more if each hand had more tricks or the hidden card were sometimes revealed, so that long-range inference mattered more than the deal.

From 3 play(s): 255 choices, 255 with a reason given (about 61.7 words each); results {'seat 3': 3}.

## Reasoning: 3 of 3

Most card-play decisions rest on exact counting of what is played, turned and hidden, and players regularly infer voids, where the hidden card is, and what opponents hold. Players also use the public bids to predict whether opponents will try to win or duck a trick, which is the inference the designer said drives play.

- match 2, turn 77, seat 2: "Seat 3 played 10C under my KC instead of winning with the AC, so the AC is almost certainly the hidden card." (Infers the hidden card from an opponent's choice, given that opponent's bid.)
- match 1, turn 30, seat 2: "If either opponent holds JS (2/3 chance), they must follow suit and beat my 10S, so I make my bid exactly." (Weighs two lines by explicit odds over the unseen cards.)
- match 3, turn 70, seat 3: "both opponents have shown they are void. The 10C must be the hidden card." (Deduces the hidden card from voids shown earlier in the hand.)
- match 3, turn 14, seat 3: "Only one card is hidden, so at least one opponent holds AS or JS. They must follow suit, and either card beats my 10S." (Uses the single hidden card to guarantee a losing lead later.)

## Planning: 3 of 3

Players often give up a trick on purpose and keep master cards, so that they land exactly on their bid over the remaining tricks. Some plans depend on what opponents are forced or motivated to do, such as making a rival overshoot their bid or handing over the lead to someone who needs tricks.

- match 1, turn 42, seat 2: "Whatever seat 3 leads next, I can win one trick with AD and one with QS. Trumping now would win a trick worth 0 because of the JC" (Declines a trick to take exactly two later. The plan was carried out at turn 45.)
- match 3, turn 43, seat 3: "So I give up 10C here and keep QS, KC and QC. On the next trick I can drop QS when I'm void in the led suit. KC and QC then win the last two tricks, giving exactly 3." (Three-trick plan to hit the bid exactly, followed through at turns 45 and 48.)
- match 2, turn 23, seat 1: "If I play JH, seat 2 wins with QH. That trick scores 2 − 4 = −2 for them, and it gives them a third trick, so they miss their bid of 2." (Plans the rest of the hand around forcing an opponent over their bid.)
- match 1, turn 57, seat 3: "If I play 10D here, seat 2 wins this trick. That puts seat 2 at 3 tricks, over their bid. I then win the last trick with 10C" (Two-trick plan that uses the opponent's forced win.)

## Luck: 2 of 3

The deal and the turned trump decide what is biddable, and some last tricks depend on where the hidden card lies. However, the exact-bid bonus let weak hands score, and most hands were decided by bidding accuracy and careful play.

- match 1, turn 28, seat 3: "It wins only if AS is the hidden card, which is a 1 in 5 chance." (The hidden card can decide whether a bid is made, though players reason about the risk.)
- match 3, turn 77, seat 2: "I'm far behind (6 against 25), so winning outright is very unlikely" (A run of weak deals left one seat out of contention.)
