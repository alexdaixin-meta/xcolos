# Reasoning and planning: Speicherstadt Queues (Even Rotation)

**Verdict: adequate.** Winning takes real reasoning: inferring rivals' colours, reading bluffs, and computing the price each queue position can produce. Planning is moderate, mostly worker and coin reservation over one or two rounds. Because the margins are so thin, the timing of contract colours against priority swings results. Revealing the deck order further ahead, or letting priority be bid for, would reward longer-term planning more and reduce the role of the draw.

From 3 play(s): 492 choices, 398 with a reason given (about 39.0 words each); results {'seat 3': 1, 'seat 2': 1, 'seat 1': 1}.

## Reasoning: 3 of 3

Most decisions rest on specific, correct inference. Players deduce rivals' contract colours from purchase patterns and prices paid, and expose bluffs using the rule that contracts are unique. They work out the highest price their queue position can produce, and they count the cards left of each colour. Talk is used deliberately to misdirect, which matches the designer's core task of identifying rivals' colours and hiding one's own.

- match 1, turn 6, seat 2: "Seat 4's claim to Blue must be false, since every contract is different." (Uses the unique-contract rule to expose a rival's bluff about Blue, the player's own colour.)
- match 2, turn 24, seat 2: "Seat 1 paid 3 and then 2 for two Purples, which suggests Purple is their contract and worth 5 each to them." (Infers a hidden contract from prices paid and bids to deny it.)
- match 2, turn 71, seat 2: "Six of the 36 cards are Green, and 5 have already appeared. I hold 4 of them, so only 1 Green is left in the deck." (Card counting used to decide that further concealment of the colour matters little.)
- match 1, turn 89, seat 3: "If all four seats queue, I'd be offered 4, which is too expensive, and seat 4 behind me says it will block at 3." (Works through the descending-price queue and relies on the next blocker in line.)

## Planning: 2 of 3

Players routinely keep a worker free for a later lot in the round and hold coins for contract goods expected later. Some carry plans across rounds, such as buying filler now because they expect to be blocked next round, or keeping coins for a block in the final round. Most decisions are still single-round value calls, and multi-round plans that depend on other players stay fairly short.

- match 2, turn 148, seat 3: "Last round brings two Oranges and a Yellow, and the other seats have said they will queue on the Oranges to deny me. That makes cheap filler this round worthwhile." (Adjusts this round's play to an expected block next round.)
- match 2, turn 150, seat 2: "I'll still have about 10 coins for next round's Oranges, where I plan to block Seat 3." (Keeps coins for a planned block one round ahead; the block was then carried out in round 12.)
- match 1, turn 148, seat 4: "That still leaves enough to buy the last Purple next round, when I'm first in line." (Budgets around a known future priority position.)
- match 2, turn 132, seat 4: "Remaining cards are 2 Yellow, 2 Orange and 2 Red, so I still have chances at Red in the last two rounds." (Uses the remaining deck to plan future purchases.)

## Luck: 2 of 3

Final margins were only 1-5 points, so it mattered a great deal whether your contract colour came up in rounds where you led priority. Several end-game results were settled by that timing. Choices still shaped who bought what, through blocking and price-raising, so luck decided some results rather than all of them.

- match 1, turn 137, seat 3: "I'm first in line this round with 13 coins, so I win any lot I queue on whatever the others do." (The last two Greens arrived while their holder led priority, so the deciding goods could not be blocked.)
- match 2, turn 158, seat 3: "I am last in priority, so I can only ever be offered an Orange at price 1." (The last Oranges arrived with their holder last in line, which let the others block them and decided the match.)

- match 1, turn 91, seat 1: The player put a second worker on Blue at limit 2, sitting behind two declared blockers ahead in the queue. That left the Yellow on Lot 3 unqueued, and it was discarded. A limit-1 worker on Yellow would very likely have won a +1 good in a match lost by 3 points.
