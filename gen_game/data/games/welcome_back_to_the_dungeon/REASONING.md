# Reasoning and planning: Dungeon Dare

**Verdict: adequate.** Winning takes real reasoning about hidden cards, the put-back mechanic, and who is left holding the dungeon, plus multi-turn add-then-exit and stalling plans; the narrowest runs still came down to the two unseen deck cards. The game would demand more if runners had any partial information about the deck cards, or if passing carried a cost, so that the add-then-bail pattern was less dominant.

From 3 play(s): 348 choices, 348 with a reason given (about 36.2 words each); results {'seats 1 and 3': 2, 'seat 3': 1}.

## Reasoning: 3 of 3

At the key decisions players reason from specific hidden-information clues: which card was passed back on top of the deck, whose claims were proven lies at the reveal, how many 4–6 monsters remain, and who wins under each outcome. Routine ADD turns are more formulaic ('Torch kills it, adding marks me'), but the pass, sacrifice and endgame choices rest on correct, state-specific inference and rough odds.

- match 2, turn 44, seat 1: "Seat 3 claimed a Goblin, then passed, so the card I just drew, a 4, was theirs: they lied." (Uses the put-back mechanic to work out another player's hidden card and catch a bluff.)
- match 1, turn 28, seat 1: "The Dragon I just drew is probably the card seat 3 passed on." (Infers an opponent's private draw from their pass.)
- match 1, turn 108, seat 2: "If the pile really is two Goblins, I estimate about a 50% chance of success this way, against about 42% if I add the Lich." (Weighs options by estimated odds, conditional on doubted claims.)
- match 1, turn 104, seat 1: "Any failed run then gives me at least a tie, and an outright win if seat 3 is the one who runs." (Maps each possible runner to the final scoring outcome.)

## Planning: 3 of 3

Players run multi-turn plans: add a Spear-proof Dragon, later strip the Spear, then pass; or stall to turn 40 for a guaranteed shared win, which depends on the opponent staying in. These plans were announced and carried out over several turns, though some backfired when opponents passed first.

- match 2, turn 24, seat 2: "Later I can sacrifice the Spear myself to make the pile deadly, then pass and collect a point if the runner fails." (A three-step plan, partly executed at turn 36.)
- match 2, turn 36, seat 2: "I plan to pass on my next turn." (Carries out the earlier plan and states the next step.)
- match 1, turn 144, seat 1: "If seat 2 stays in, I can keep adding every turn until turn 40. The dungeon then ends unscored, and seat 3 and I tie on 3 to share the win." (Long-horizon stall plan that relies on the opponent's choices and the turn limit.)
- match 1, turn 56, seat 2: "If seat 3 passes as they say, I run with good odds. If seat 3 stays in, I keep the option to pass later" (A contingent plan over the next turns.)

## Luck: 2 of 3

The shuffle decides which monsters each player can add and which two deck cards a runner faces. Several runs were decided by one or two points (10 vs 10, 9 vs 10), and one of those decided match 2. Skill still steered who ended up as runner, which was usually the decisive choice.

- match 2, turn 68, seat 2: "If I pass, my Vampire goes back on top of the deck and seat 3 has to face it." (Seat 3 then survived with 9 damage against 10 HP. That narrow outcome, set by the unknown second card, gave seat 3 the points that won the match.)
- match 2, turn 92, seat 3: "the remaining deck likely holds several of those." (The win hinged on the unseen deck composition.)

## Weak spots

- match 2, turn 36, seat 2: Seat 2 stripped the Spear to make its own Dragon lethal while staying IN, with two other players still in who could both pass first. They did, and seat 2 ran its own deadly pile. Seat 2 should have made the pile deadly only when it could exit next, or avoided the trap by passing once it was already marked added.
- match 1, turn 56, seat 2: One message earlier, seat 2 judged the run likely to fail and planned to pass. It then added a Vampire and stayed in on the strength of seat 3's unreliable message, and ended up running a failing pile for the third time. Passing as an added player was the better-reasoned line.
- match 1, turn 20, seat 2: Seat 2 added the Lich, expecting about a 95% fail, while still IN and with no escape plan. It ended up as runner of the pile it had poisoned. Sacrificing an item, or making sure it could pass before others did, would have been safer.
