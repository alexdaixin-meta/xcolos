# Reasoning and planning: Trischettn Duel

**Verdict: adequate.** Winning this game clearly rewards counting and endgame planning: players who tracked the visible talon pickups could read the opponent's whole hand and sequence the last 8 tricks to capture Aces and the last-trick bonus. Because the endgame becomes almost deterministic, the deal often fixes the outcome; more hidden information late in the deal, or scoring that is less dominated by who holds the top cards, would make skill matter more.

## Reasoning: 3 of 3

Almost every decision rests on specific card tracking: players place the talon cards the winner took in view, infer voids from discards, and after the talon empties they reconstruct the opponent's exact hand and compute the pip outcome of each line. A few slips appear, such as a wrong claim that two discards score the same and a risky Ace lead into a known unseen Nine, but the inference is mostly correct and concrete.

## Planning: 3 of 3

Players repeatedly make multi-trick plans that depend on the opponent's forced follows: they order leads so the last-trick bonus falls their way, keep guard cards, and set up discard squeezes. These plans are stated in advance and then carried out over several tricks.

- match 1, turn 49, seat 2: "Win this trick.
- Lead Ten of Hearts: they must play the King of Hearts, and I win.
- Lead Nine of Leaves: it beats their King or Ober of Leaves.
- Lead King of Acorns: it beats their Ober of Acorns.
- Lead Unter of Acorns last" (A five-trick plan relying on forced follows, executed exactly to secure the draw.)

## Luck: 2 of 3

Once the talon empties, play is close to fully determined by the cards held, so the deal and draws largely set how many pips each side could reach. In match 3 deal 2, seat 1 held only low Bells and Acorns against seat 2's high cards, and no lead could stop seat 2 from taking every trick after trick 10. Choices still moved results at the margin, for example the match 3 Ace-of-Leaves discard and the match 1 Ace-of-Acorns lead.

- match 3, turn 43, seat 1: "So they win this trick and every trick after it, whatever I play, and take all 19 remaining pips... No lead changes this" (The card distribution decided the endgame regardless of choice.)
- match 1, turn 17, seat 2: "Every card I could lead is beaten by a card they must or can play. Once they win the lead, I can never win a trick back." (The rest of the deal was locked in by the holdings.)

## Weak spots

- match 1, turn 33, seat 2: Led the Ace of Acorns into the unseen Nine of Acorns to chase the talon Nine of Bells. Two turns earlier the same player had said this lead risked 3 pips. The opponent captured it, a 4-pip swing that also handed over the lead and the Nine of Bells. A cheap probe such as the Unter of Leaves or Unter of Bells would have risked far less.
- match 3, turn 26, seat 2: Claimed 'the pips come out the same either way' when choosing between discarding the Ten or the Ace of Bells. Discarding the Ace gives 3 pips instead of 1 and leaves only the Ten to win the last trick, a 2-pip swing each way. The player kept the Ace, which was correct, but the stated reasoning was wrong.
- match 2, turn 4, seat 1: Led the Seven of Leaves with no knowledge of the opponent's Leaves, which let the opponent cash the Ace of Leaves for 3 pips plus the talon Ober. Leading from a suit where the top cards were already gone, or holding the lead with a safe winner, would have avoided handing over a free Ace.
- match 1, turn 26, seat 2: The stated choice (Ober of Leaves) does not match the card actually played (Ober of Bells). It made no difference here, but the decision and the play were inconsistent.
