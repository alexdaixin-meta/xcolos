# Reasoning and planning: Wizard Duel of Three

**Verdict: adequate.** Winning clearly rewards card counting, reading the sealed bids, and timing Wizards and trumps over several tricks, and the players did this well and often. The deal still sets much of the ceiling; more hands, or letting players see some bids before bidding, would give choices more weight relative to the cards dealt.

From 3 play(s): 216 choices, 216 with a reason given (about 58.7 words each); results {'seat 2': 2, 'seats 1 and 2': 1}.

## Reasoning: 3 of 3

Most decisions rest on specific card tracking: which higher cards are gone, that the turned card is out of play, and which rivals have made their bids and will now duck. Players also estimate odds explicitly. This matches the designer's focus on reading revealed bids to decide who will take or dodge a trick.

- match 1, turn 60, seat 2: "About 8 of the 18 unseen cards beat R3 (R5–R7, B2, B5, B6 and both Wizards). The chance neither opponent holds one is roughly (10/18)² ≈ 31%." (Explicit odds from unseen cards used to choose between winning now and winning later.)
- match 1, turn 35, seat 2: "Y6 was the turned card, so it is out of play, and Y5 is in my hand. That means the only cards that can beat Y4 are Y7 or a Wizard" (Uses the turned card and their own hand to see that two trumps are equivalent, so the lower one is spent.)
- match 3, turn 67, seat 3: "On the first trick seat 1 couldn't beat my B1, which suggests they hold no blue and no trump" (Infers a rival's hidden holdings from their earlier play.)
- match 2, turn 28, seat 1: "Leading B5 first gives roughly a 43% chance of making my bid. Leading G6 first gives roughly 20%." (Compares lead orders by the chance of making an exact bid.)

## Planning: 3 of 3

Players routinely keep Wizards and top trumps for a chosen moment and order their leads across the remaining tricks. Several plans depend on rivals' incentives, such as letting a rival fill their bid so that rival will then duck. These plans were usually carried out as stated.

- match 1, turn 37, seat 2: "Leading my lowest card, B1, should give seat 3 the chance to win this trick. Once seat 3 has its trick, both opponents will avoid winning more. I can then take my second trick with Y5" (A multi-trick plan that relies on a rival's bid-driven behaviour.)
- match 3, turn 70, seat 1: "If I lead B2 now, they can easily throw off-suit cards... On the final trick they each have one card and are forced to play it. So I lead G5 now and W1 next, keeping B2 for the end." (Sequences three tricks so that 0-bidders are forced to play on the last one.)
- match 1, turn 12, seat 1: "In trick 4 I'll play third and see every card before choosing. That lets me take exactly one trick with W2 and dump B1 safely" (Plans the timing of the Wizard around future seat position.)
- match 1, turn 42, seat 2: "If I play G4 here instead, seat 3's Y2 wins. That pushes seat 3 past their bid of 1. I keep Y5, a high trump, to win one later trick." (Ducks to keep a trump for later while breaking a rival's bid.)

## Luck: 2 of 3

Hand strength from the deal, such as one seat drawing both Wizards, and which unseen cards rivals happened to hold decided a number of bids. Exact-bid scoring meant weak hands could still score, but the margins in matches 1 and 3 owe much to the cards dealt.

- match 1, turn 66, seat 2: "I bid 2 and hold both Wizards, which should give me my two tricks." (A dealt hand that made the bid nearly automatic.)
- match 2, turn 37, seat 1: "Seats 2 and 3 both still need tricks, so they will try to win whatever I lead." (A sound inference undone by rivals' unseen Wizards, whose owners dumped cards instead, so seat 1 overtook its bid.)

## Weak spots

- match 2, turn 8, seat 3: Bidding 0 on a Jester lead, the player judged G3 and Y1 'about equally likely to be beaten'. Y1 loses to every other yellow, so it was the clearly safer card to set the suit with.
- match 3, turn 19, seat 3: With a bid of 1, W1 in hand and two 0-bidders at the table, the player led R1 expecting to lose. Both 0-bidders would naturally duck under it, so the low lead was likely to win and start an overtrick chain, which ended in 4 tricks. The player did not consider that both rivals would duck.
- match 3, turn 25, seat 3: Already over the bid, the player led G5 into two players who had bid 0 and would duck, so the trick was near-certain to win. Leading R3, the card more of the unseen cards could beat, was no worse here and kept G5 for a spot where it could be discarded.
