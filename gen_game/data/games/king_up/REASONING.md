# Reasoning and planning: King Up: Fair Throne

**Verdict: strong.** Winning came down to endgame No-card counting and timing who makes the final push, which does reward inference and a few rounds of planning. The first 12 rounds were a collusive leveling ritual with no decoys. More would be demanded by removing the fixed last-pusher advantage, for example by randomizing the round-16 raiser, and by making the goal-revealing raise pattern costly.

From 3 play(s): 379 choices, 379 with a reason given (about 30.7 words each); results {'seat 4': 2, 'seats 1, 2, 3 and 4': 1}.

## Reasoning: 2 of 3

Through rounds 1-12 the reasons are almost all the same line, "raise my own letter, it fits the plan." Reading rivals' goals was trivial because nobody raised a decoy. In the endgame the reasoning sharpens: players count No cards from the anonymous tallies, and seat 4 correctly worked out that all 8 cards were gone. Still, there are clear mistakes in the counting.

- match 1, turn 126, seat 4: "The No votes so far add up to 3 + 3 + 2 = 8, and that is all 8 cards at the table." (Correct inference from the anonymous No counts that nobody can veto, which won the game.)
- match 1, turn 120, seat 2: "Across the two earlier votes, seats 1, 3 and 4 spent five No cards out of six, so only one of them has a card left." (Specific card-counting about the other seats' hidden resources.)
- match 2, turn 112, seat 2: "B is almost certainly seat 3's character, and seats 1 and 4 both have a strong reason to block it." (Voting Yes to free-ride on rivals' vetoes and keep a card, as the designer intended.)
- match 2, turn 127, seat 2: "I hold the last No card: I voted Yes on D and B and No on E, and 7 of the 8 No cards are now spent." (Tracked its own spending and the table's to see it alone could force the draw.)

## Planning: 2 of 3

Most of the game is a mechanical even-pass ritual with no look-ahead. The last four rounds bring real multi-round plans: a pushing rotation, timing who raises first, and steering one's own letter to the final, unvetoable slot. Seat 4 in match 1 carried such a plan through to a win.

- match 2, turn 94, seat 1: "If the others then level H and K, I raise first next round. That lets me choose which character gets pushed to 5, and I can pick one that isn't mine." (Sets up control of the first forced push one round ahead.)
- match 1, turn 115, seat 1: "I'll keep that quiet and steer the rotation so my letter comes up last, where a full Yes vote would win it for me." (Multi-round plan that relies on others exhausting their No cards.)
- match 1, turn 125, seat 4: "Nobody can vote No now, so the letter I push to 5 will be crowned. I'll keep the message neutral." (Hides the coming defection in talk so the final raise can be carried out.)
- match 2, turn 126, seat 4: "Sticking to the agreed plan is the more reliable path to at least a shared result." (Weighs the final push against what the last card-holder will do.)

## Luck: 1 of 3

The deal is symmetric and was not decisive. Results turned on No-card exhaustion and on who is first raiser in round 16. That is fixed by seat order (seat 4 every time with 4 players and 16 rounds), not by chance, and it gives the last seat a structural edge the designer claims was removed.

- match 2, turn 125, seat 4: "Final round, and I raise first. I have no No cards left" (The final push always falls to seat 4, a structural position rather than a random one.)

## Weak spots

- match 1, turn 119, seat 3: Claimed "Seat 1 never cast No on J," but J drew 3 No votes while its owner seat 2 voted Yes, so seats 1, 3 and 4 all voted No. This misread fed the wrong card count used at turn 121.
- match 1, turn 121, seat 3: Spent the last No on D, believing seat 2 had none, although seat 2 still had one. Holding it would have left a veto for round 16 and stopped seat 4's free crowning of E.
- match 1, turn 104, seat 3: All three rivals vetoed J and later G (3 No votes each) when one suffices. This redundant spending exhausted the table and handed the last pusher a guaranteed king. Voting Yes when others clearly would veto, as seat 2 did in match 2, was the better-reasoned option.
- match 1, turn 29, seat 4: "Hiding my goal gains little now" shows the pattern across all seats: no decoy raises at all, so every goal was public by round 4. Decoying, one of the designer's core skills, was never tried.
- match 3, turn 101, seat 1: Pushed its own letter E to 5 under a "push your own" deal, guaranteeing its own loss first. Pushing a rival's letter also loses one letter for that seat and keeps seat 1's own letters safe.
