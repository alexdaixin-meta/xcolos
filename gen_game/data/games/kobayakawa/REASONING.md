# Reasoning and planning: Kobayakawa Duel Rounds with Table Talk

**Verdict: adequate.** Winning takes real odds reasoning about K, discards and the standings, plus some endgame planning. The deal still decides many rounds. The game would demand more if honesty history and KEEP/SWAP signals carried more weight, for example through more rounds with persistent card information or payoffs that reward reading opponents over raw card strength.

From 3 play(s): 356 choices, 326 with a reason given (about 46.8 words each); results {'seat 2': 3}.

## Reasoning: 3 of 3

Most decisions rest on specific, correct counting: K against the hand, which cards are gone through discards and the face-up K, and catching impossible claims because the speaker holds that card. Inference from table talk and KEEP/SWAP signals is thinner than the designer intends: claims are often waved off as 'unreliable' rather than weighed, and a few errors slip through.

- match 1, turn 47, seat 3: "I hold the 9, so seat 1's claim of keeping a 9 is false." (Uses own hand to expose a false claim.)
- match 2, turn 82, seat 2: "against one opponent, only an 8 or 9 wins, because as lowest card they reach 13 or 14." (Exact case analysis against the remaining cards after counting discards.)
- match 1, turn 36, seat 4: "Low cards have every reason to fight this round, so I'd most likely lose my stake." (Accounts for which hands choose to fight, not just random odds.)
- match 3, turn 34, seat 2: "Seat 1 discarded the 14, so only a 15 can beat me." (Uses a public discard to narrow the threats.)

## Planning: 2 of 3

Within each round, players choose talk to shape who fights. Across rounds, leaders compute when passing locks the win, and trailing players deliberately seek variance. Multi-round plans that depend on rivals' behaviour are rare, and the leader in match 1 broke his own lock-in plan.

- match 1, turn 85, seat 2: "Passing every round locks in the win, so the draw choice doesn't matter." (Endgame calculation over the remaining rounds.)
- match 1, turn 116, seat 3: "Passing keeps my 8, which seat 1 can at most tie." (Plays for final placement against a specific rival.)
- match 1, turn 20, seat 4: "Playing it down should draw others into a fight." (Talk set up to shape the coming fight poll.)
- match 3, turn 61, seat 1: "I'm far behind (2 kamons against seat 2's 14), so I want a riskier hand." (Changes draw strategy according to the standings.)

## Luck: 2 of 3

A fresh deal every round decides who holds the dominant card. Seat 2's early leads in matches 1 and 3 came largely from holding 15, 13 and 1 at the right K. Choices about when to fight and when to sit out still mattered at the margins and in the endgame.

- match 1, turn 46, seat 2: "I hold a 15, the highest card, and K is only 2." (A four-way pot was decided by the deal.)
- match 3, turn 95, seat 3: "My card is 1, the lowest possible, so I am always the lowest fighter." (A dealt card made the showdown unlosable.)

## Weak spots

- match 2, turn 9, seat 1: Took seat 4's 'kept a 10' claim at face value. Seat 4 KEPT when K was 14, which strongly signals a low card. Weighing that signal should have made the 5 look riskier.
- match 1, turn 5, seat 1: Believed 'I cannot lose a showdown' with 14 at K=1. In a three-way fight where a lower card takes K, a 15 beats the 14, and that is exactly what happened.
- match 1, turn 93, seat 2: Had just calculated that passing locks the win, then fought with a 4 at K=13 against seat 3. Seat 3 KEPT and was talking up a fight, both hints of a very low card. Seat 2 lost a stake for no gain.
- match 2, turn 94, seat 2: Listed 8 among the cards that beat the 14, but 8 was the face-up K and could not be held.
